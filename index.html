What Am I?

You cannot read your own identity. Everyone else can — and they have every reason to lie about it.

3–8 players · 15–25 min · no install, no account

The one rule

You cannot read your own card.

Three of those cards are not yours, so you can read them. The gold one is yours. You will never see its face — not by lifting it, not by tilting it, not by asking the game nicely.

You are the only person at the table who does not know what you are.

Your turn — pick one

Ask, swap, or declare.

Ask. Name a player and ask them what you are. They answer with a role name.

Swap. Spend a token to push your card into the central pile and take a new one off the top. The table watches you change. You still cannot see the new one.

Declare. Name your own role out loud and turn over your card.

You start each round with two swap tokens and two of them is usually one too many.

Why anyone ever lies

Every answer is against the speaker's interest.

🜁
Tell the truth
and they declare on their next turn, score, and win. You handed them the match.
🜂
Lie
and if they declare wrong they must name whoever misled them. You go out with them.
🜃
Say nothing
and the round dies at the pile, worth zero, and nobody wins anything. So nobody stays quiet.

That triangle is the whole game. Everything players invent to escape it — trading answers, cross-checking two askers, deliberate vagueness, silence as leverage — is the actual play.

Winning

Declare right and take the points.

Each role is worth its number, 1 through 8. Declare correctly and you score it. First to 5 points takes the match.

Declare wrong and you are out — and you must name the player whose answer you relied on. They go out with you. If you worked it out yourself, only you go out.

An eliminated player still gets a WITNESS card: one public accusation, true or false, that nobody can punish them for. Leaving the table does not mean leaving the game.

The part that makes it a game

You can see most of the deck. Not all of it.

Twelve cards, four players. You can read three of them, so your own card is one of the other nine — and eight of those are face-down in the middle where nobody can see them.

What you could deduce right now
Fool ×2 Guard ×2 Widow ×1 Monk ×2 Oracle ×1 Judge ×1 Prince ×1 Queen ×1

Ask someone. Burn a swap. Watch what other people are told and what they do about it. The pile shrinks. Late in a round the set becomes small enough to solve, and that is when the lying gets serious.

Rules

What Am I?

Setup. Everyone gets one card, face-out — readable by the whole table except its owner. Four cards go face-down in a central pile. Two swap tokens each.

Because the pile is only four cards, your own face is always one of five you cannot see. That is what makes asking worth doing: two honest answers usually pin it down to one.

  • Ask — name a player and ask what you are. They answer with a role name, and they may lie.
  • Swap — spend a token. Your card goes to the bottom of the pile, you take the top one face-out. The table knows your new identity. You do not.
  • Declare — name your own role. Right: score its value. Wrong: you are out, and you name whoever misled you — they go out too.

Telling the truth pays. Every player who answered you honestly — and whose answer you then acted on — scores a point when you declare correctly. So honesty is a strategy, not a sacrifice, and the liar is the one taking the risk.

Ending. The round ends when someone declares correctly. It takes two full circuits of silence before the table gives up and declares simultaneously — the first lap is for asking, the second is for acting on what you learned.

Match. First to 8 points.

Tell the truth and you both gain. Lie to the player about to win, and hope nobody checks.
