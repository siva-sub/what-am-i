# WHAT AM I?

**Pitch:** You cannot read your own identity. Everyone else can. They have every reason to lie about it, and you have to act before someone else guesses theirs.

**Players:** 3–6 · **Time:** 15–25 min · **Components:** 12 role cards, 2 tokens per player, a central pile

No abilities. No board. No arithmetic. All depth comes from people.

---

## Setup

- Shuffle the **12 role cards**: Monk, Assassin, Widow, Prince, Guard, Oracle, Fool, Judge, and four duplicates. Each role carries a value from 1 to 8 (printed).
- Deal **one card to each player, face-out**, so that everyone at the table can read it except its owner.
- **Never let a player read their own card.** Do not tell them. Do not confirm. They may not lift it, tilt it, or use a phone camera.
- The remaining cards become a **face-down central pile** (6 cards at a 6-player table).
- Each player takes **2 swap tokens**.

## Turn — do exactly one

**1. Ask.** Name one player and ask "what am I?" They must answer with a role name. The answer may be true or false, and they are allowed to lie. You may ask again on later turns, and you may ask the same person twice.

**2. Swap.** Spend a token. Push your card face-down into the central pile, draw the top card, and hold it face-out. **The table now knows your new identity and you do not.**

**3. Declare.** Name your own role out loud. The table reads your card.

- **Correct →** you score that role's value in points.
- **Wrong →** you are out of the round. Additionally, you must name the player whose answer you relied on. **That player is also out.** If you relied on nobody, only you are out.

## Ending the round

Play until someone declares correctly. **If a full circuit of the table passes with no declaration, everyone declares simultaneously** by pointing at a role on the reference list. All wrong declarers are out, and each names whoever misled them. If nobody is correct, the round goes to the pile and is worth zero.

Match: first to **5 points**. Rounds take about three minutes.

---

## Why this works

**Truth has no incentive, so it must be extracted.** Telling another player their role costs you the game outright — they will simply declare and win. So nobody volunteers the truth. But a **lie is punished by citation**: if you declare wrong and name your source, that source is eliminated with you. The two rules together create the only interesting choice in the game:

> Tell the truth and lose. Lie and risk elimination. Say nothing and nobody wins.

Everything players invent to escape that triangle — trading answers, testing consistency across multiple askers, deliberate ambiguity, silence as leverage — is the actual game.

**The design decision that saved it.** My first draft dealt the full deck, one card per player. That **dissolves instantly**: you see the other five cards, the set is known, and your own card is the sixth by elimination. You never need to ask anyone anything. The game kills itself on turn one.

The fix is the **central pile**. With 12 cards and 6 players, you can see 5 of them, so your identity is one of 7 unknowns. Asking is now the only way to narrow it, and every answer is a chance to be misled. It also means careful players who track what has been revealed genuinely know more than careless ones.

**The second trap it avoids.** If lying were free, nobody would ever tell the truth and the game would stall. If lying were instantly checkable, nobody would ever lie. Citation-punishment puts lying at *cost* rather than at *blockade*, which is the band where bluffing games live.

## Variants

**Duel (2 players).** Drop to 8 cards, 2 face-down in the centre. Alternate asking and declaring. The liar is the whole game.

**Big table (7–8).** 16 cards, 8 in the centre. Wait times rise, so add: you may ask two questions per turn but must declare by your third.

**Abilities (adds weight, loses purity).** Give each role one line: Monk protects a player from being swapped, Oracle may look at one central card, Judge may force a swap, Assassin may eliminate a player who has declared wrong twice. Only add this after the base game works.

**No-declaration variant (harsher).** Nobody may declare before round three. Forces information trading before anyone can win, and makes the citation rule bite harder.

---

## Playtest questions

1. Does anyone ever get a truth for free? If truth-telling happens at all outside the endgame, the citation penalty is too weak.
2. Do players actually trade ("tell me mine, I'll tell you yours")? That trade is the game's heart, and if it never appears, the incentive triangle is not landing.
3. After a wrong declaration, is the cited liar's elimination satisfying or arbitrary? If arbitrary, the citation must be written down when the answer is given, not chosen after the fact.
4. Does the central pile ever get ignored? If yes, reduce the role values so declining to declare is not a viable stalling strategy.
5. At what table size does the game stop being about reading people and start being about waiting for a turn?
